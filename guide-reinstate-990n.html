How to Reinstate a Revoked 501(c)(3) After Missing Form 990-N

The four IRS reinstatement paths, what each one requires, and the deadline that decides which one you get · Updated November 2026

If you just learned your organization has been automatically revoked — maybe a grant portal flagged it, or a donor's accountant couldn't find you on the IRS exempt list — take a breath. This is common, it is almost always recoverable, and for a small nonprofit the path is usually the simplest and cheapest one the IRS offers. What follows is the plain-English version of how reinstatement works and the one date that matters most.

First, confirm it. Look your organization up on the IRS Tax Exempt Organization Search and find your revocation date and the date you were added to the Auto-Revocation List. Those dates determine which reinstatement path you qualify for. (Not sure whether you're actually revoked, or just late? Our free status checker reads the IRS record for you in a few seconds.)

Why status gets revoked — and why it's fixable

Federal law automatically revokes the tax-exempt status of any organization that fails to file its required annual return or notice for three consecutive years. For most small nonprofits the required notice is the Form 990-N (e-Postcard). Because the 990-N carries no late fee, nothing warns you along the way — the status simply lapses in the background. More than half a million organizations have been revoked this way, so the IRS built a defined reinstatement process, spelled out in Revenue Procedure 2014-11. There are four paths, and which one applies to you depends mostly on your size and how fast you act.

The four reinstatement paths

Fastest · for small nonprofits

1. Streamlined retroactive reinstatement

This is the path most 990-N filers use. You qualify if your organization was eligible to file the 990-N or 990-EZ for each of the three missed years, this is your first automatic revocation, and you apply within 15 months of the later of your revocation letter or your posting to the Auto-Revocation List.

You file Form 1023-EZ (or Form 1023) with the user fee, write "Revenue Procedure 2014-11" at the top, and — importantly — no reasonable-cause explanation is required. Status is restored retroactively to the revocation date, so the gap effectively disappears.

Retroactive · within 15 months

2. Retroactive reinstatement (within 15 months, larger filers)

If you were required to file a full Form 990 or 990-EZ (not just the 990-N) but still apply within the 15-month window, you can get retroactive reinstatement by including a reasonable-cause statement for one of the three failure years, plus the exemption application and fee.

Retroactive · after 15 months

3. Retroactive reinstatement (after 15 months)

Miss the 15-month window and retroactive relief is still possible, but the bar is higher: you must provide a reasonable-cause statement for all three missed years, explaining what happened, how you discovered it, and the safeguards you've put in place so it won't recur — along with the application and fee.

Simplest · not retroactive

4. Reinstatement from the postmark date

If you don't need (or can't support) retroactive relief, you can simply apply and be reinstated going forward, effective the date the IRS receives your application. It's the least paperwork, but the revoked period stays a gap — meaning gifts made during it generally aren't deductible.

Step by step

  1. Confirm the revocation and note both the revocation date and Auto-Revocation List date on the IRS Tax Exempt Organization Search.
  2. Check your state standing. You generally don't need to re-incorporate — just confirm the entity is in good standing with your state.
  3. Pick your path from the four above, based on your size and whether you're inside the 15-month window.
  4. File the back 990-N notices for the missed years (and any required 990/990-EZ returns). Note: don't attempt to e-file a full Form 990 while revoked — the IRS will reject it.
  5. Submit Form 1023-EZ or 1023 with the user fee and, if your path requires it, the reasonable-cause statement.
  6. Wait for the new determination letter — then put a system in place so you never repeat the three-year slide.
This is general reference information, not legal or tax advice, and the streamlined route in particular has eligibility limits. If your situation is unusual — a prior revocation, large returns due, or a complicated gap — a nonprofit attorney or CPA is worth the consult.

After you're reinstated: don't let it happen twice

Here's the part worth sitting with: the streamlined, forgiving path only works once. A second automatic revocation can't use it, and reinstatement the second time is slower and harder. Reinstated organizations lose status again for the same reason they lost it the first time — not the form, but the forgetting, usually in a year when the treasurer or board changed and the handoff dropped.

Common questions

Does 990 Autopilot handle the reinstatement application for me?
No — reinstatement is a one-time exemption application (Form 1023-EZ or 1023) that you or a CPA/attorney file with the IRS. What we do is the part that comes after: file your annual 990-N automatically every year so your reinstated status stays protected.
How do I know if I'm inside the 15-month window?
Count 15 months from the later of your IRS revocation letter date or the date you appeared on the Auto-Revocation List. If you're inside it and were a 990-N/990-EZ-eligible organization, the streamlined path (no reasonable-cause letter) is usually available.
Can I still accept donations while revoked?
You can accept gifts, but they generally aren't tax-deductible for the donor until you're reinstated. Retroactive reinstatement to the revocation date restores deductibility for the gap period, which is a big reason to aim for a retroactive path when you qualify.
Is there a penalty for the missed 990-N years?
There's no monetary penalty for late 990-N notices themselves. The cost of missing them is the revocation and the user fee to come back — not a per-form fine.
