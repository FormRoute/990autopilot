How to Start a 501(c)(3): Steps, Cost & Timeline

What it actually takes to turn a cause into a tax-exempt nonprofit — in plain English · Updated November 2026

Starting a 501(c)(3) isn't as daunting as it looks. For most small organizations it comes down to a handful of concrete steps, a few hundred dollars, and a short IRS form. Here's the whole path, what it costs, how long it takes, and where small nonprofits trip up.

The steps

  1. Pick a name and a purpose. Choose a name that's available in your state, and write a clear charitable purpose — educational, religious, scientific, charitable, and so on. This becomes the backbone of your application.
  2. Form a nonprofit corporation in your state. File Articles of Incorporation with your Secretary of State. Crucially, the Articles must include specific IRS language — a purpose clause and a dissolution clause (assets go to another tax-exempt organization if you close). Missing these is the most common reason applications stall.
  3. Get an EIN. Apply for a free Employer Identification Number from the IRS. It takes a few minutes online and costs nothing.
  4. Adopt bylaws and a conflict-of-interest policy, and hold your first board meeting. Most applications assume you have a board (often at least three unrelated directors) and basic governance in place.
  5. Apply for federal tax exemption. File Form 1023-EZ (the streamlined form most small nonprofits can use) or the full Form 1023, through Pay.gov, and pay the IRS user fee.
  6. Handle state-level steps. Depending on your state, you may need state tax exemption and charitable-solicitation registration before you fundraise.

How long it takes

State incorporation is usually fast — days to a couple of weeks. After you submit, the 1023-EZ is typically approved in about 2–4 weeks for small, clean applications; the full 1023 can take several months. So a small nonprofit using the 1023-EZ can often be fully recognized within a month or so of applying.

Is the fast 1023-EZ right for you?

Most small nonprofits qualify for the streamlined 1023-EZ. You generally can use it if you expect gross receipts of $50,000 or less, have assets under $250,000, and aren't an excluded type (churches, schools, hospitals, and a few others use the full 1023). You confirm this on the IRS's eligibility worksheet before filing.

Get the Articles right the first time. The single most common reason a 1023-EZ gets held up or rejected is Articles of Incorporation that are missing the IRS-required purpose and dissolution language. Fixing it after the fact means amending your state filing — easier to get right up front.

After you're approved: don't lose it

Getting the status is the hard part; keeping it is where many new nonprofits quietly fail. Every year you must file an annual return — for most small organizations that's the Form 990-N — and missing it three years running triggers automatic revocation. The risk is highest in the early years, when no one's thought about it yet. Common questions

Do I need a lawyer to start a 501(c)(3)?
Not necessarily. Many small nonprofits complete the process themselves or with a preparation service. Legal help is most useful for complex organizations or ones that can't use the 1023-EZ.
What's the difference between 1023-EZ and 1023?
The 1023-EZ is a shorter, cheaper ($275) streamlined application for small nonprofits; the full 1023 ($600) is longer and required for larger or excluded organization types. We can tell you which fits you.
