Does a Nonprofit With No Income Have to File Form 990-N?

The short answer is yes — and the reason it matters more for a quiet year than a busy one · Updated November 2026

It feels backwards. The organization raised nothing, ran no programs, maybe didn't even hold a meeting — so why would the IRS want a filing? But this is exactly the situation that quietly revokes more nonprofits than any other. A tax-exempt organization's annual filing obligation doesn't pause because the organization did. Here's what you actually owe in a $0 year, and the small number of real exceptions.

Yes — you still have to file

Once the IRS has recognized your organization as tax-exempt, you must file an annual return or notice every year, regardless of income or activity. For a small nonprofit — gross receipts normally $50,000 or less — that annual filing is the Form 990-N (e-Postcard), and it is still required in a year with $0 in receipts and nothing happening. The 990-N isn't a tax return; it's the IRS's way of confirming you still exist and are still small. A quiet year is simply a 990-N that confirms the quiet.

This is the classic trap. A board goes dormant — the founder steps back, activity pauses "for a year or two" — and everyone assumes no money means no paperwork. Three quiet years later, the status is automatically revoked, and the group discovers it the moment someone tries to restart and apply for a grant.

What "no income" means here

The filing turns on gross receipts — the total coming in before expenses. If that's $0, you're obviously under the $50,000 line and file the 990-N. If a dormant organization still collects a little (a small recurring donation, bank interest, leftover dues), that's still gross receipts, and as long as it's normally $50,000 or less, the 990-N is still the right form. Either way, the obligation is the same: file the short notice, on time, every year.

The few real exceptions

A handful of organizations genuinely don't have to file the 990-N:

  • Churches and certain church-affiliated organizations are generally not required to file.
  • Organizations covered by a group return filed by a central/parent organization (the parent files on their behalf).
  • Private foundations (they file Form 990-PF instead) and a few other specialized entities.

If you're not clearly in one of those buckets, assume you must file — and if you're unsure whether a parent organization covers you, confirm it rather than guess. Guessing wrong here is what leads to revocation.

How to protect a dormant or low-activity nonprofit

The whole risk is a forgotten filing in a year when no one's paying attention — which is precisely when a dormant organization is most exposed. You can file the 990-N yourself for free at IRS.gov each year; the only requirement is remembering to, every single year, even the boring ones.

Common questions

We had literally $0 this year. Still file?
Yes. The 990-N is required even with zero receipts and zero activity. It confirms your organization still exists and is still small — missing it counts as a missed year toward revocation.
We've been inactive for two years and didn't file. Are we in trouble?
Not yet revoked — that takes three consecutive missed years — but you're close. File the current year's 990-N now and get back on track. Here's what's at stake.
We already got revoked while dormant. Can we come back?
Usually yes. Small organizations can often be reinstated, sometimes retroactively. See how reinstatement works.
Is our nonprofit maybe covered by a parent's group return?
Some chapters and affiliates are included in a central organization's group return, which would cover the filing. Don't assume it — confirm with the parent organization, because if you're not actually covered, the missed years still count against you.
