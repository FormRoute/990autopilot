What Happens If You Miss Form 990-N?

What the automatic-revocation rule actually means for a small nonprofit · Updated October 2026

Short version: nothing happens the first time, or the second — and that's the trap. The Form 990-N (e-Postcard) has no late fee, so a missed deadline sends no bill and no alarm. The danger is what the IRS does quietly after three straight misses.

The three-year rule

Under federal law, any tax-exempt organization that fails to file its required annual return or notice for three consecutive years has its tax-exempt status automatically revoked. For small nonprofits, "required notice" means the 990-N. The revocation is automatic — there is no judgment call and, in practice, no warning letter telling you it's about to happen.

The IRS reports that well over half a million organizations have lost their status this way since the rule took effect. The overwhelming majority didn't choose to stop — they simply forgot, usually in a year when the person who used to handle it moved on.

Why it's so easy to miss: no late fee means no pain, no reminder, no deadline pressure. Three quiet years later, the status is gone — and the first sign is often a donor's accountant asking why the organization isn't on the IRS exempt list anymore.

What revocation actually costs you

  • Donations stop being deductible. Your donors can no longer claim a charitable deduction for gifts made after the revocation date. Grant-makers typically won't fund a revoked organization.
  • You may owe income tax. Once exempt status is gone, the organization can be treated as a taxable entity and may have to file corporate returns.
  • You land on a public list. The IRS publishes the Auto-Revocation List, and it shows up in the same databases donors and grant portals check.
  • Reinstatement takes money and months. You apply again using Form 1023-EZ or Form 1023, with an IRS user fee of $275 for the 1023-EZ, plus the weeks of preparation and IRS processing time.

How reinstatement works (if it already happened)

If your status has been revoked, you can apply to get it back. Organizations that were eligible to file the 990-N or 990-EZ for the years that caused the revocation may qualify for retroactive reinstatement to the revocation date — but generally only if you apply within 15 months of your revocation letter or your appearance on the IRS list, and only if you haven't been auto-revoked before. The mechanics:

  1. Confirm the revocation on the IRS Tax Exempt Organization Search and note the date.
  2. File the back 990-N notices for the missed years.
  3. Submit Form 1023-EZ (or 1023) with the user fee and a reasonable-cause statement if required.
  4. Wait for the IRS to process and reissue your determination letter.

It's recoverable — but it's exactly the kind of money, paperwork, and board stress a $29 annual filing is meant to prevent.

Common questions

Is there a penalty for filing the 990-N late?
No monetary penalty for the 990-N itself. The consequence is structural — three consecutive missed years triggers automatic revocation.
If I'm one year behind, am I in trouble?
Not yet. Filing now still counts, and one or two missed years do not revoke your status. What matters is never reaching three in a row.
Will the IRS warn me before revoking?
Don't count on it. Revocation for non-filing is automatic once the three-year threshold is met, and organizations routinely report learning about it only afterward.
How much is reinstatement?
The IRS user fee is $275 for Form 1023-EZ (more for the full Form 1023), plus the time to prepare and file the application and back notices.
